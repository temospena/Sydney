Objectives

How does cycling network expansion impact…

  • Trip efficiency: duration, distance, circuity ⏱️
  • Safety: Level of Traffic Stress exposure 🛡️
  • Accessibility: 15-min potential to reach opportunities 🏫
  • Continuity: number of interruptions when using CI 🛑

Goals:

  • Analyze 10 years of evolution (2016–2026) across 50 cities
  • Estimate model elasticities of infrastructure improvements
  • Distinguish effects by infrastructure type (protected, painted, sharrows, shared foot)

OD Pairs Sampling

  • 10km buffer from city center coordinates

  • 20,000 OD pairs generated per city

  • Destinations randomly sampled, weighted by building volume (proxy for trip attraction)

  • Distance follows a log-normal function calibrated for cycling trips (Kou and Cai 2019)

  • Origin hexagons (with buildings) sampled using H3 resolution 9 (~200m edge length, ~400m “diameter”, ~0.105 km²)

Trip distance distribution

  • Log-normal calibration ensures routes resemble realistic urban cycling trips.

→

About 75% of trips are below 5 km. This already accounts for ~40% circuity.

CI Classification in 4 types

With osmactive custom classification of CI, we classified OSM segments into 4 simplified types to assess infrastructure quality, based on OSM tags:

Type OSM examples
Protected (separated) highway:cycleway → separated tracks
Painted (medium) cycleway:lane → bike lanes on road
Sharrows (weak/mixed) cycleway:shared_lane → advisory
Foot (shared w/pedestrian) highway:pedestrian & bicycle:yes → shared paths 


-- Conceptual query (based on osmactive logic)
CASE WHEN highway = 'cycleway' OR cycleway IN ('track', 'opposite_track') THEN 'Protected'
     WHEN cycleway IN ('lane', 'opposite_lane') THEN 'Painted'
     WHEN cycleway IN ('share_busway', 'shared_lane') OR bicycle_road = 'yes' THEN 'Sharrows'
     WHEN highway IN ('path', 'footway', 'pedestrian') AND bicycle IN ('yes', 'designated')
          THEN 'Foot'
END AS ci_type

Level of Traffic Stress (LTS)

  • Classification from Mineta Transport Institute (2012)

LTS - Alta Planning

  • Adapted by Conveyal (Conway 2015) to applied to every OSM edge as:
If way does not allow cars → LTS 1
If highway:residential / living_street → LTS 1
If maxspeed < 40 km/h AND lanes < 4 → LTS 2
If highway:tertiary/unclassified + cycleway tag → LTS 2
If cycleway tag on busier road → LTS 3
Otherwise → LTS 4

r5r routing package

r5r R package (Pereira et al. 2021) for bicycle routing, relies on LTS of the highways (and not exactly CI):

library(r5r)
trips <- detailed_itineraries(
    r5r_network = r5_engine,   # city × year street network
    origins = origins_hex,     # same od pairs used across all years
    destinations = dests_hex,
    mode = "BICYCLE",
    max_lts = lts_level,       # 1, 2, 3, or 4
    osm_link_ids = TRUE        # match segments with LTS
)
  • max_lts limits route choice to low-stress paths.
  • If no connected low-stress route exists: R5 penalises the segment by setting speed to walking pace (3.6 km/h) instead of cycling (12 km/h) → 3× time penalty (cost).

Accessibility – 15-min potential

  • For each origin (H3 hex), we compute the sum of building volumes reachable within a 15-minute cycling trip.
  • Increases when more destinations become reachable, or existing destinations become fast enough to reach with low stress.

Modelling

Fixed Effects Panel Regression across Macro (city-wide) and Micro (route-level) scales.

Macro Level (City-Wide)

  • How do massive, city-scale expansions affect overall averages?
  • Two-way Fixed Effects (city + year), LTS 1 and LTS 2 subsets.

\[\ln(Y_{ct}) = \beta \cdot \ln(\text{Total CI}_{ct}) + \gamma \cdot \ln(\text{Road km}_{ct}) + \alpha_c + \tau_t + \epsilon_{ct}\]

Effective sample: 51 cities × 5 years = 255 observations per LTS level. Standard errors clustered by city.

Note

Key statistical note: The main independent variable (km of CI) varies only at the city-year level, even though routing outcomes are averaged over 20,000 routes. The effective degrees of freedom are lower than the observation count suggests.

The Accessibility Story

“Building bike lanes doesn’t necessarily make trips faster; it makes them possible, and it makes them safe.”

  • Duration effect: small but real – network growth saves time
  • Accessibility effect: larger – new CI bridges previously uncrossable barriers (arterials, rivers, highways), unlocking thousands of new destinations within a safe 15-min ride

If a city expands its network by 50%, accessibility for cautious riders jumps by ~9% — thousands of new jobs and services within a safe 15-minute ride.

Micro Level (Route)

  • ~9.9 million LTS 1 route observations (50 cities × 5 years × 20k ODs).
  • Fixed effects at route × year level — each OD pair controls for itself.

\[ \ln(Y_{irt}) = \beta_1 \text{Protected}_{rt} + \beta_2 \text{Painted}_{rt} + \beta_3 \text{Sharrows}_{rt} + \beta_4 \text{Foot}_{rt} + \alpha_{ir} + \tau_t + \epsilon_{irt} \]

Where \(\text{Protected}_{rt}\) = km of protected CI along route \(r\) in year \(t\).

Outcomes modelled:

  • Log duration, log circuity
  • % of route at LTS 1 (safety)
  • Weighted average LTS score
  • Number of CI interruptions (gaps > 100m)

The Commuter Trade-off 🔄

Per 1 km of protected CI built:

  • Physical circuity increases by ~6%
    (rider detours to use the safe path)

  • Effective trip duration decreases by ~1.3%
    (safer route avoids slow mixed-traffic friction)

Cyclists willingly accept a longer detour to achieve a faster, safer trip.

This is a strong behavioural signal: cyclists value perceived safety over raw distance.

The Network Glue 🔗

Impact on route interruptions (gaps > 100m without CI):

  • Protected CI: −0.084 per km (significant) ✅
    → Closes real gaps in the network
  • Painted lanes: −0.055 per km (border signif.) ⚠️
  • Sharrows: +0.055 per km (not signific., may increase gaps)

Protected infrastructure is the ONLY type that significantly reduces network fragmentation.

Paint and sharrows do not provide the connectivity “glue” needed to stitch together a continuous, low-stress network.

The Arterial Lure 🛣️

Stress penalty (Avg. LTS increase) by trip distance and infrastructure type:

Key insight: Paint and sharrows on arterial roads dramatically increase stress penalties for short/medium trips. Protected infrastructure neutralises the stress from arterial crossings across all trip lengths.

The “Fragmentation Phase” Paradox ⚠️

At the macro level, total CI positively predicts interruptions (β ≈ +0.26, ***):

Each 10% expansion of total CI increases average interruptions per route by ~2.6%

Why? Cities in 2016–2026 were in the “Fragmented Network Phase”:

  1. No CI (Stage 1): Rider goes fully in mixed traffic → 0 interruptions.
  2. Fragmented CI (Stage 2): Isolated “islands” of safe lanes; each new isolated segment creates 2 new gap edges where the cyclist must enter/exit mixed traffic.
  3. Mature network (Stage 3): Connected corridors → interruptions drop.

This is a data-confirmed policy finding — not a modelling flaw:

“Just building raw kilometres doesn’t create a cohesive network. It creates a fragmented, stressful riding experience.”
The policy recommendation: shift success metrics from “km built” → “gaps closed”.

Findings

Key Takeaways

Finding Metric Effect
Network growth saves time Avg. duration (macro) −0.66% per 10% CI growth
Network unlocks destinations 15-min accessibility +1.75–1.97% per 10% CI
Detour accepted for safety Circuity (micro) +6% per 1km protected CI
But trip is effectively shorter Duration (micro) −1.3% per 1km protected CI
Only protected CI closes gaps Interruptions −0.084 per km (signif.)
More CI → more gaps (fragmented era) Interruptions (macro) +2.6% per 10% CI ⚠️

The central policy message:
Infrastructure works. But isolated corridors without gap-closing create a fragmented, stressful network. The win lies in continuity, not just quantity.

Limitations

Routing Engine

  • Calm residential roads (LTS 1 by default) are treated the same as dedicated CI → engine may route through dense neighbourhoods rather than lanes.
  • Custom cost schemes in R5 are subjective; standard LTS was used to maintain reproducibility and comparability.

OSM Data Quality

  • Tagging evolution: OSM segments are tagged differently across years — our algorithm captures some of this, but tagging changes ≠ physical infrastructure changes (e.g., Munich 2021→2026).
  • Contributions growth: OSM data density in all cities increased 2016→2026 — some “new CI” may just be newly mapped old CI.
  • Segment fragmentation: Protected CI drawn as separate segments is realistic, but increases with tagging evolution.

Endogeneity ⚠️

  • Cities don’t build bike lanes randomly — they build where demand is highest or space is cheapest.
  • This biases elasticity estimates upward unless Instrumental Variables (IV) are applied (e.g., historical railway lines, planned-but-unfunded corridors).
  • Current results should be interpreted as associations, not causal effects.

These routing-engine and OSM limitations would affect any study using these data sources — they are inherent to using open, longitudinal data globally.
