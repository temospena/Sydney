Modelling

Fixed Effects Panel Regression across Macro (city-wide) and Micro (route-level) scales.

Recall the Question

What are the systemic and route-level impacts of adding 1 km of cycling infrastructure on trip performance, safety, and accessibility?

  • Scope: 50 global cities × 10 years (2016–2026)
  • Data: Historical OSM networks + Global Building Atlas (LoD1)
  • Simulation: 20,000 OD pairs per city (~5M routes total), routed by R5 using Level of Traffic Stress (LTS)

This is a longitudinal spatial panel, not a cross-sectional snapshot. We are watching cities grow over a decade.

Methodology

Two-Scale Fixed Effects

Macro Model (City-Wide)

Systemic impact of network expansion on city averages.

\[\ln(Y_{ct}) = \beta_1 \cdot \ln(\text{Total CI}_{ct}) + \beta_2 \cdot \ln(\text{Total Road}_{ct}) + \alpha_c + \tau_t + \varepsilon_{ct}\]

  • \(Y_{ct}\): city-wide average outcome (duration, accessibility) in city \(c\), year \(t\)
  • \(\alpha_c\): city fixed effects — absorbs unobserved time-invariant city traits (geography, sprawl)
  • \(\tau_t\): year fixed effects — absorbs global shocks (COVID infrastructure boom, OSM tagging growth)
  • SE clustered by city; N = 250 obs (50 cities × 5 years)

The log-log specification gives us elasticities directly: a 1% increase in X → β% change in Y. The FE approach means we are not comparing Amsterdam to São Paulo; we watch each city change relative to itself.

Micro Model (Route-Level)

Impact of infrastructure on individual route characteristics.

\[Y_{irt} = \beta_1 \text{Prot}_{rt} + \beta_2 \text{Paint}_{rt} + \beta_3 \text{Sharrow}_{rt} + \beta_4 \text{Foot}_{rt} + \gamma_{ir} + \tau_t + \varepsilon_{irt}\]

  • \(Y_{irt}\): transformation depends on the outcome:
    • log for duration & circuity → semi-elasticity
    • level for % LTS1, avg. stress score, interruptions → marginal effect
  • \(\text{Prot}_{rt}\), etc.: km of CI type along route \(r\) in year \(t\) (always in levels)
  • \(\gamma_{ir}\): route fixed effect — compares the same OD pairs routes accross the years
  • \(\tau_t\): year FE — absorbs global OSM tagging trends
  • SE clustered by city; N ≈ 9.9M route-year observations

The route fixed effect is the key advantage. We are not comparing a flat downtown trip to a hilly suburb. We are watching the exact same OD pair change as the city builds infrastructure. This controls for all time-invariant route geometry.

Before we proceed…

1. Duration = Network Impedance, not Physical Speed
Time savings in our models represent the elimination of network deterrence, not bikes riding faster.

2. OSM Tagging ≈ Physical Reality
In OSM, painted lanes are tags on existing arterial road segments, while protected paths are separate geometries. This mirrors reality:

  • Paint → rider shares the arterial’s space → inherits its danger
  • Protection → spatially separate → implies a road diet or space-reallocation (lanes, parking, etc.)

Macro Results – Interpretation

Tip

Elasticity interpretation (log-log):
A 10% expansion of the city’s CI network…

  • reduces average LTS 1 trip duration by ≈ 1.38% \((-0.145 \times \ln(1.10))\)
  • increases 15-min accessibility by ≈ 3.28% \((+0.344 \times \ln(1.10))\)

A 50% expansion → accessibility jumps by ≈ 14% — thousands of new safe destinations.

“Building bike lanes doesn’t only make trips faster — it makes them possible and safe.”

  • Duration effect: real — network growth eliminates routing deterrence
  • Accessibility effect: large — CI bridges arterials and rivers, unlocking destinations previously unreachable within a safe 15-min ride

Micro Results – Finding 1: The Commuter Trade-Off 🔄

Per 1 km of protected CI built:

  • Physical circuity +~6%
    (rider detours to use the safe path)

  • Effective trip duration −~1.3%
    (safer route avoids slow mixed-traffic friction)

Cyclists willingly accept a longer detour for a faster, safer trip.

Strong behavioural signal: perceived safety over raw distance.

Micro Results – Finding 2: The Network Glue 🔗

Impact on route interruptions (gaps > 100m without CI):

  • Protected CI: −0.084 per km (significant) ✅
    → Closes real gaps in the network
  • Painted lanes: −0.055 per km (border signif.) ⚠️
  • Sharrows: +0.055 per km (not significant)

Protected CI is the ONLY type that significantly reduces network fragmentation.

Micro Results – Finding 3: The Arterial Lure 🛣️

Stress penalty (Avg. LTS increase) by trip distance and infrastructure type:

Key insight: Paint and sharrows on arterials spike stress penalties, especially for short trips.
Protected infrastructure neutralises arterial stress at all trip lengths.

Robustness – Interpretation

Setup: We forced the routing engine to treat all CI (including paint and sharrows on busy arterials) as fully safe (LTS = 1).

Result: Under the alternative, differences between CI types collapse — paint and sharrows reduce stress as effectively as protected tracks.

Conclusion:

Our primary findings are not a routing algorithm artefact.
Cities inherently place opportunistic paint on dangerous arterials.
Without physical protection, the rider absorbs 100% of that arterial danger.
Physical separation is the only intervention that eliminates the stress penalty.

Note

This answers the toughest critique: the “Arterial Lure” is driven by real-world infrastructure placement, not a modelling error.

The “Fragmentation Phase” Paradox ⚠️

At the macro level, total CI positively predicts interruptions (β ≈ +0.26, ***):

Each 10% expansion of total CI increases average interruptions per route by ~2.6%

Why? Cities in 2016–2026 were in the “Fragmented Network Phase”:

  1. No CI (Stage 1): Rider uses mixed traffic → 0 interruptions
  2. Fragmented CI (Stage 2): Isolated islands; each new segment creates 2 new gap edges
  3. Mature network (Stage 3): Connected corridors → interruptions drop

Policy implication: Shift success metrics from “km built” → “gaps closed”
